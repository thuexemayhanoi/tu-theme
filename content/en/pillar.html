  • Confident riders who are at ease in dense traffic and hold a licence that is valid for motorcycles in Vietnam.

Ride-hailing apps and taxis are easy to use in Hanoi, so a rental makes sense when it gives you something they don't:

  • Rent a bike — if you want several stops a day, your own timetable, rides beyond the centre or daily transport for weeks on end.

Scooters and motorbikes for rent

Mr Tu rents the two types of bike you see everywhere on Hanoi's streets. Both are light, easy to park and easy to refuel.

Automatic scooters: twist and go

No gears, a step-through frame and storage space under the seat. An automatic is the easy choice for city riding, for carrying a passenger in slow traffic and for riders who are new to Vietnam's roads.

Semi-automatics: gears without a clutch

You shift with your foot, but there is no clutch lever to manage. Semi-automatics are light and simple, and they suit riders who have used one before and like choosing their own gear.

  • Two people, or a day bag on every ride — an automatic scooter with room under the seat; ask which model suits two riders.

Documents and licence: what you need

As of October 2026, the rules are set by Vietnam's Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety (No. 36/2024/QH15). In brief:

  • Which class — motorcycles up to 125 cc need an A1 licence and bigger bikes an A licence, both available from age 18. A car-only licence does not count.
  • If you are visiting — your home motorcycle licence is valid only together with an International Driving Permit (IDP) issued under the 1968 Vienna Convention. A permit issued under the 1949 Geneva Convention is not covered, so check which one you hold.
  • Helmets — required for rider and passenger, made to the national standard and worn with the strap fastened.
  • Insurance — every bike must carry compulsory third-party insurance, and rental bikes do. That cover does not protect you or the bike, so make sure your travel insurance includes motorbike riding.

For the rental itself, bring your passport or national ID and your licence, plus your IDP if you use one. Mr Tu checks them for the agreement and settles the deposit separately.

Riding safely in Hanoi

Hanoi traffic looks chaotic at first, but it follows its own rhythm. These habits help you ride with it rather than against it:

  • Helmet on, strap fastened — for rider and passenger, on every ride, however short.
  • Go with the flow — hold a steady line and speed, and avoid sudden swerves or stops. Other riders steer around you based on what you are doing now.
  • Use the horn to be seen — a short tap at junctions or when overtaking simply tells others you are there. It isn't considered rude.
  • Read the signs, not just the map — many central streets are one-way, and some roads are closed to motorbikes.
  • Avoid rush hour at first — weekday mornings and late afternoons are the busiest times, so take your first rides outside them.
  • Slow down in the rain — roads turn slippery and visibility drops. Brake early and carry a rain poncho.
  • Set your route before you ride — check the map while parked, and pull over rather than handling your phone on the move.
  • Riding two-up — your passenger needs a helmet too, and the bike handles differently with two people: brake earlier and take corners more gently.
  • Keep your documents on you — carry your licence, and your IDP if you use one, every time you ride.
  • No alcohol before riding — not even one drink. The law treats riding after drinking severely.
  • Park where it's allowed — use attended, paid parking in the centre and keep the ticket.
