Automatic scooter or semi-automatic?

Both are everyday transport in Vietnam. The real difference is whether you change gear yourself, and that matters most in heavy traffic.

Automatic scooters

Twist the throttle and go. With no gears and no clutch, you can keep your attention on the traffic, which makes an automatic the natural choice for Hanoi's stop-start streets and your first days on Vietnamese roads. Models such as the Honda Vision, Honda Air Blade, Honda Click and Yamaha Mio are automatics; the list above shows which ones Mr Tu currently offers. Most have a compartment under the seat, handy for a raincoat.

An automatic suits you if:

  • you'll mostly ride in the city and around the Old Quarter
  • you're an experienced rider but new to traffic in Vietnam
  • you'll often carry a passenger around town

Semi-automatic motorbikes

A semi-automatic such as the Honda Wave has gears you change with your foot, but no clutch lever. It takes a little practice, and in return gives you more control: you can hold a low gear on a climb and let the engine slow the bike on the way down. It is the everyday workhorse for many people in Hanoi.

A semi-automatic suits you if:

  • you already know how to ride with gears
  • you like having more control over the engine
  • you'll be commuting every day for a month or more and are used to geared bikes

New to motorbikes?

Be honest with yourself here. Hanoi's traffic is dense and unpredictable, and it is not the place to learn to ride. You'll also need a licence that covers motorcycles. If you have ridden scooters at home and hold the right licence, an automatic is the gentlest way to start.

Engine size and your licence

In Vietnam, licence class A1 covers two-wheel motorcycles up to 125 cc, and class A covers anything larger. Some models come in more than one engine size, so ask Mr Tu which version you're getting and make sure your licence covers it.

Visitors can ride on a Vietnamese licence, or on their national motorcycle licence backed by an International Driving Permit issued under the 1968 Vienna Convention. Our guide to renting a motorbike in Hanoi explains the rules in full.

Helmets for rider and passenger

Helmets are compulsory in Vietnam for everyone on the bike. They must meet the national standard and be worn with the strap fastened, even for a five-minute ride. Tell us how many people will ride, and Mr Tu will confirm a helmet for each of you before you book.

At hand-over, check that each helmet fits:

  • it sits level on your head and covers your forehead
  • it feels snug and doesn't move when you shake your head
  • the strap closes firmly under your chin, with room for about two fingers

The condition check at hand-over

Before you ride away, you and Mr Tu check the bike together and photograph its condition. What you find goes into the written rental agreement, so you both have the same record when the bike comes back.

  • brakes, front and rear
  • tyres: tread, pressure and any damage
  • lights: headlight, brake light and indicators
  • horn and mirrors
  • fuel level, noted in the agreement
  • existing scratches or dents, photographed from every side

Fuel and filling up

The scooters and semi-automatics run on petrol. At hand-over, ask which grade to use and where the fuel cap is, and make sure the fuel level is noted in the agreement. Petrol stations in Hanoi are staffed: pull up at the pump, open the cap and tell the attendant how much you'd like, or ask for a full tank. Fuel costs during the rental are yours.

Choosing a bike: common questions

Which bike is easiest for a first ride in Hanoi?

An automatic scooter, provided you're already a confident, licensed rider. With no gears to think about, you can focus on the traffic around you.

Can I reserve a specific model?

You can ask for one. Mr Tu confirms whether that model is free for your dates when you contact us, and if it isn't, you can choose another bike from the list.

Which licence do I need for these bikes?

One that covers motorcycles of the right engine size: class A1 up to 125 cc, class A above that. If you're visiting, your home motorcycle licence counts only when you also carry an IDP issued under the 1968 Vienna Convention.
